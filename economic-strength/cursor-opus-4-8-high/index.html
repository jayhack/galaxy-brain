Two Economies, One Country

By almost every aggregate and international yardstick the United States has the strongest large rich-world economy on the planet. By the yardstick most Americans actually use — what a paycheck buys, week to week — the typical household has run hard and stood still for half a decade. Both things are true. That tension is the whole story.

The honest verdict: the U.S. economy is objectively strong and globally dominant — yet structurally unequal and, right now, re-squeezed by a fresh inflation spike. Growth is increasingly carried by the wealthiest fifth of households riding a record stock market. For most people, "the economy" and "my economy" have quietly become two different things.

The Central Paradox

Good macro, bad micro

The numbers that economists watch and the numbers that households feel have come apart. The economy is still growing, jobs are plentiful, and stocks are at records. Yet consumer sentiment sits near the lowest level ever recorded, and for the third straight month, more than half of consumers volunteered "high prices" as the thing dragging down their finances.

Why It Feels Bad — Part 1

The price level, not just the inflation rate

Economists cheer when the rate of inflation falls. Households live on the level of prices, which never fell — it just stopped rising as fast, and is now re-accelerating. After the 2021–23 surge, a renewed spike in mid-2026 (driven by energy) pushed annual CPI back to 4.2%, the highest reading since 2023.

Wages have roughly kept pace over the long run but are losing the latest round: average hourly earnings rose 3.4% over the year to May 2026 while prices rose 4.2% — so the typical worker's real hourly pay is shrinking again, right now. Year-ahead inflation expectations sit at an elevated 4.6%, well above anything seen in 2024.

Why It Feels Bad — Part 2

Five years of running in place

The single most important fact about the typical American's finances: inflation-adjusted median household income is essentially the same as it was before the pandemic. The economy got bigger; the median household did not get richer.

Real median household income (2024 dollars)

The midpoint U.S. household. Adjusted for inflation, it has gone nowhere in five years.

Other household-level strain shows up in the borrowing data. Total household debt hit a record $18.8 trillion in Q1 2026 (up $4.6T since 2019). Credit-card delinquency transitions are near levels last seen during the 2008 financial crisis, and student-loan serious delinquency has spiked to 10.3% as pandemic-era forbearance ended. Housing and mortgages, by contrast, remain the strongest part of the consumer balance sheet.

The Structural Story

The K-shaped economy

Aggregate spending looks healthy because the people at the top are spending more than ever. Strip the average apart and you find two trajectories: the top fifth pulling away on the back of a record stock market, and everyone else barely treading water against inflation.

Whose spending grew (past year)

Annual growth in consumer outlays vs. the 4.2% inflation line.

Concentration at the top

Top 20% now drive ~60% of all consumer spending; the top 1% hold ~32% of net worth.

For the bottom 80% of earners, spending power has not outpaced inflation in six years. The economy's growth now rests on "a small group of the well-to-do" whose confidence tracks their stock portfolios.

This is why the expansion feels fragile: the S&P 500 is up ~9% year-to-date in 2026 and the Nasdaq ~14%, propelled by an AI-driven megacap rally near record highs. That wealth effect is doing real work in the spending data — which also means a market correction would hit the economy's main engine first.

How America Compares

Richest in the world — at a price

Zoom out to the global picture and American pessimism looks puzzling. The U.S. is the richest large economy on Earth, and the typical American household has more money to spend than its peers almost anywhere. But the country buys strikingly little health for what it pays.

This is the international mirror of the domestic paradox: enormous aggregate wealth and high take-home incomes, paired with worse outcomes in the things — health, security, the sense of getting ahead — that shape how prosperity is actually experienced.

The Bottom Line

So, are ordinary Americans in hardship?

The honest answer is "it depends who, and it depends what you compare to." Mass hardship of the 2008 or 2020 kind is not what the data show. A broad, grinding squeeze on the bottom 80% — real, and getting worse at the margin — is.

The case that it's strong

  • GDP still expanding; the U.S. has outgrown every G7 peer since 2019.
  • Unemployment 4.3% — historically low and stable for a year.
  • Median household income at/above its pre-pandemic peak.
  • Median take-home income 2nd-highest in the world, ahead of all big European economies.
  • Household net worth and the stock market at record highs.
  • Housing affordability improving (slightly) for the first time since 2022.

The case that it's strained

  • Prices ~29% above 2020 and re-accelerating; CPI back to 4.2%.
  • Real wages shrinking again right now (pay +3.4% vs prices +4.2%).
  • Median household income flat in real terms for ~5 years.
  • Bottom 80% haven't beaten inflation in 6 years; record $18.8T debt; card & student-loan delinquencies rising.
  • Growth dangerously dependent on top-20% spending tied to stocks.
  • Pays double for healthcare yet lives 3.7 years less than peers.

The United States in mid-2026 is a genuinely rich, resilient economy with a distribution problem and a fresh inflation problem. The aggregate is healthy; the median is stuck; the bottom is slipping; the top is soaring. Whether you call that "strong" or "hard" depends almost entirely on where you stand in it — which is exactly why the country can't agree on the answer.
